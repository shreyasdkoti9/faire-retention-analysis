Expansion is masking churn: a plan to fix retailer retention

TL;DR: Retailer retention at Faire is decided in the first 30 days and runs on brand discovery. We recommend two experiments — nudge brand discovery and expand Net60 terms — worth up to ~$4M/yr in GMV, with the first launching this quarter.

Background

Analysis of Faire retailers who placed their first order in June–July 2020 (7,400 retailers, $129.1M confirmed GMV) shows net dollar retention dipping to 49% in month 5, then rising to 87% by month 16 — which on the surface looks positive. However, NDR masks retailer churn with expansion: Faire is seeing steady retailer churn, with the surviving base declining by ~2 points every month, while retailers that survive order more and more frequently, thereby raising GMV. Left alone, this compounds: 43% of a cohort’s retailers will never order again by month 12. The goal of this document is to put forth a prioritized set of initiatives to improve retailer retention at Faire.

Underlying assumption: the data contains no promotion or macro-event fields, and we cannot fully correct for COVID-era distortion of the June–July 2020 baseline or holiday-2021 seasonality. We therefore treat within-cohort comparisons (segment vs segment, early behavior vs outcomes) as robust, and absolute tail-month NDR levels as directional only.

Key business trends

1. Expansion masks churn: NDR recovers while retailers keep leaving. 19% of retailers order only once, and permanent attrition then compounds at ~2pp per month. The Net Dollar Retention "recovery" decomposes as survivors (about a third of stores active in any given month) spending 2.4x more — expansion outrunning churn, not retailers returning. And that growth comes from ordering more often (2.3 → 5.1 checkouts per active month), not bigger baskets.

2. Faire is a discovery marketplace. Of every dollar spent after the first month, only 15 cents is a reorder from the brands a retailer started with — 47 cents is a first-ever purchase of a new brand, and 38 cents is repeat buying of brands discovered along the way. Holding early engagement constant, single-brand starters whose early repeat included a new brand retain at 63% vs 50% for same-brand-only (adjusted OR 1.90). Retention here is linked to brand discovery, not reordering.

3. Net60 behaves like a retention product, pending causal proof. Net60 retailers finish at 116% NDR vs 54% for payment-on-shipment — the largest dollar split in the data. Flexible payment terms go hand in hand with both staying longer and spending more, and the effect concentrates in order frequency (+47%) rather than basket size (+8%) — the working-capital fingerprint. Underwriting selection is real (4 of 24 matched strata reverse), hence experiment before rollout.

The problem statement

Expansion masks churn: NDR recovers while retailers keep leaving

NDR is a product of two effects: how many stores still buy (unit survival) × how much each surviving store spends. The two can move opposite ways and cancel — and here they do.

The survival line bleeds ~2pp every month (81% at month 1 → 57% at month 12) while spend per surviving store climbs 2.4x ($1,487 → $3,565). NDR's "recovery" to 87% is the second effect outrunning the first — not retailers coming back.

Key trend 1 → Recommendation 1: nudge second-brand discovery

Faire is a discovery economy, not a reorder pipe

Of every dollar spent after the first month, only 15 cents is a reorder from the brands a retailer started with. 47 cents is a first-ever purchase of a new brand, and 38 cents is repeat buying of brands discovered along the way — by month 16, first-month reorders are down to 8% of spend. 13,852 brands, top brand 1% of GMV.

Initial opportunity sizing: single-brand starters average $9.3k downstream GMV vs $16.4k for 2–5-brand starters — a $7.1k gap. Controls suggest roughly 60% of that gap survives adjustment (a 40% selection haircut), and converting 20% of the 3,703 single-brand starters gives 741 × ~$4.3k ≈ $3.2M over 17 months. Upper bound until the RCT reads out.

The second brand carries the retention: holding early engagement constant, single-brand starters whose early repeat included a new brand retain at 63% vs 50% (adjusted OR 1.90), and starting with an anchor brand (top-quartile repeat rate) lifts retention 50.2% vs 38.9% within every spend quartile.

Key trend 2 → Recommendation 2: expand Net60 terms

Focus on payment terms: Net60 behaves like a retention product

Net60 retailers finish at 116% NDR vs 54% for payment-on-shipment — the largest dollar split in the data, and it holds in 20 of 24 size×spend strata (+13.2pp pooled unit retention).

The fingerprint says mechanism, not just selection: with identical controls, Net60's association concentrates in staying active (+70% odds) and ordering more often (+47% checkouts) while basket size barely moves (+8% AOV) — exactly the shape working-capital relief should leave. Selection is still live (4 strata reverse), which is why this ships as a randomized threshold experiment with credit guardrails, not a rollout.

Initial opportunity sizing: within matched first-month spend tiers, Net60 retailers out-spend payment-on-shipment peers by $5.1k–$12.8k downstream. Across the 585 payment-on-shipment Small/Medium retailers (the creditworthy expansion target), that is ~$4.4M over 17 months, ~$3.1M annualized — crediting only half as causal ≈ $1.6M/yr.

Seasonality is real — and June/July month 0 is a biased baseline

  • Wholesale buying seasons drive the peaks: the Jul 2021 restock and Oct–Nov 2021 holiday stock-up (amber below) land at tenure months 12–17, flattering the tail of any cohort-time NDR curve. Some of the "recovery" is the calendar, not the customer.
  • COVID re-opening summer inflates the baseline: June–July 2020 likely pulled forward unusual demand, and month 0 is mechanically inflated anyway — 100% of stores active by definition, with stock-up-sized first carts (1.39 brands vs ~1.1 after). Every NDR value is a ratio to that month, so the curve reads lower than a normal-times cohort would. Assumptions: with no promotion data, month-0 spend is treated as organic; June and July cohorts are pooled (validated — behavior is nearly identical once the calendar month is held fixed).
  • Top 3 data needs: (1) the cohort triangle — every monthly signup cohort 2019–2021 at every tenure month, cleanly splitting season from tenure; (2) a multi-year calendar GMV series to build a seasonal deflator; (3) promotion and returns feeds to test whether month 0 and the peaks are discount-driven.

Regressions: Survival vs NDR

We ran regressions to see which features show the strongest correlation with the two retention signals — Survival (how many months a retailer stays active) and NDR (how much of their first-month spend they go on to repeat). Same features, two outcomes, side by side, so you can see which lever moves which metric. Bars are standardized, so lengths are comparable within each panel.

  • Net60 terms: flexible payment terms go hand in hand with both staying longer and spending more.
  • First-month spend: the one flip — bigger first orders keep stores around longer, but a big month 0 is a high bar to beat in percentage terms, so measured NDR runs lower.
  • Browse time before converting: stores that researched longer before their first order stay longer and grow more.
  • Multi-brand start: starting with more than one brand is linked to both signals — the discovery effect.
  • gift store type: the best-fitting store type on both metrics.
  • Unknown size: skipping the size question at signup is the single strongest warning sign on both metrics.
  • food_service: restaurants and cafes drift away on both metrics — an assortment-fit issue, not a payments one.
  • Paid channel: paid-acquired stores slightly underperform on both, even after accounting for who they are.

Fit & intent: who suits Faire, and who was always going to stay

gift and mercantile_general compound on both axes — the ICP. food_service fails by shrinkage (stays, spends less). other/unknown types churn outright — partly a data-capture problem (17% of the cohort has no usable store type). And the intent signal: slow converters (>1 year browsing) are the best segment on both metrics — acquisition targeting is a retention lever that fires before day 0.

Assumptions and caveats

  • Signup month = first-ordered month: the cohort is defined by first confirmed order in Jun–Jul 2020, per the data dictionary — the definition under which Faire's reference chart reproduces exactly. Session timestamps proxy signup where timing matters, with the caveat that browsing can precede account creation and 40% of first sessions predate June 2020.
  • Exclusions — exactly two, both documented: 4 CANCELED lines (per the confirmed-order NDR definition; months 12–14, never touching the denominator) and month 18 (observable only for the June cohort). No other row deleted, no value imputed.
  • Missingness kept as signal: "Unknown" size (31%) and store type "other/unknown" (17%) are treated as segments, never imputed; retailer_business_type is free text (403 values) and unused.
  • Seasonality & baseline bias: months 12–16 coincide with Jul–Nov 2021 buying seasons; month 0 is inflated (100% active, stock-up carts, COVID re-opening). Survival values past month ~11 are right-censored and understated.

Top 3 limitations of this dataset

  • One two-month, COVID-era cohort. No neighboring cohorts means no baseline: we cannot say whether 57% month-12 survival is good, bad, or normal — and June–July 2020 re-opening dynamics may not generalize. Everything needs a modern-cohort replication before target-setting.
  • Right-censoring and month-grain dates. The window ends December 2021, so survival past month ~11 is understated (46% of dark retailers historically return, median 6 months); month-floored dates cap all cadence analysis at month granularity.
  • No supply-side, promo, returns, or credit data. We can't separate true discovery from forced switching (brands churning off the platform), test whether month-0 was promo-inflated, or split Net60's effect from underwriting selection. GMV is gross of returns and is not Faire revenue.
